Probing Recurrent Reasoning: Inside Huginn’s Hidden States

Shane JanneySeptember 2026
Read the paper ↗Explore the results ↓

This project started with a narrow question: as a recurrent language model spends more computation on the same prompt, what becomes readable in its hidden state—and how much of that information survives into the explanation it writes?

Huginn gives me an unusually clean way to ask that question because I can vary recurrence depth without changing the model weights. On my held-out set, a linear probe recovered the governing rule with 94.6% balanced accuracy at loop 18, while monitors trained on the generated rationales stayed near chance. That result is interesting, but it is not the same as showing that Huginn actually used the decoded feature. Its own task accuracy remained around chance, and counterfactual state patching produced no donor-directed answer flips in the 24 tested pairs. I therefore treat the result as evidence for late linear decodability, not as proof of a hidden causal reasoning process.

The signal I actually care about

A model’s weights are fixed during inference, but its activations change with the input and with each stage of computation. Those activations are not sentences waiting to be translated. They are high-dimensional numerical states, and the useful scientific question is usually narrower: does a particular state contain information about a property I know how to label?

Here, that property is the decision path in a controlled policy task: whether the answer is determined by the base rule or by an exception. I use a regularized linear probe to test whether that label is decodable from Huginn’s recurrent state. Then I compare that signal with what can be extracted from Huginn’s written rationale.

The observability problem

Generated explanations are useful evidence, but they are still generated text. I cannot assume that a rationale is a faithful transcript of the computation that produced the answer. Conversely, finding a feature in an activation does not mean the model used that feature. This leaves three separate questions: what the model verbalizes, what its internal state makes decodable, and what is causally involved in its behavior.

The experiment is designed around that separation. Text monitoring addresses verbalized evidence. Linear probing addresses representational evidence. Activation patching is the first causal check. Keeping those claims separate matters more to me than getting a single dramatic number.

How the recurrence works

Huginn repeatedly applies the same transformer core to an evolving hidden state. The weights stay fixed while the state changes across passes. That gives me a computational axis—recurrence depth—that I can vary without comparing different checkpoints or differently trained models.

Why GPT-6 Astra makes this question less abstract

When I began thinking about this project, the motivation was mostly methodological: compare a model’s verbalized rationale with a signal extracted from its internal state. The release of GPT-6 Astra makes that distinction much more concrete.

In the GPT-6 Astra System Card, OpenAI reports a substantial decrease in chain-of-thought monitorability relative to GPT-5.6 Sol. A major contributor is that Astra often produces shorter or less informative reasoning traces; at matched longer traces, some of the gap narrows, but not in every environment. OpenAI also reports that Astra is markedly better at controlling properties of its own chain of thought, which makes written reasoning a less stable oversight surface.

That does not mean Huginn is a proxy for Astra’s architecture, and I am not making that claim. OpenAI explicitly says its observed controllability increase does not appear to be differentially explained by architectural changes. The connection is instead at the level of the research problem: if verbalized reasoning becomes less reliable as a monitoring signal, what complementary evidence can we recover from the computation itself? OpenAI names activation monitoring as one of the alternatives it is investigating. Huginn is useful here because its recurrent state is exposed enough to let me run a small, controlled version of that question.

There is another important distinction. My “rationale” is an elicited explanation generated by Huginn; it is not equivalent to the private chain-of-thought signal OpenAI evaluates internally. The comparison on this page is therefore narrower than OpenAI’s monitorability work. I see it as a mechanistic complement, not a reproduction.

Why Huginn is a useful experimental system

The attraction of Huginn is not that recurrence is new. It is that recurrence gives me a controlled axis of test-time computation. I can run the same weights for 4, 8, 16, or 32 passes, save intermediate states, and ask when a known feature becomes linearly accessible.

If e is the prompt representation, h the recurrent state, and G the shared core with parameters θ, the update is:

hk = Gθ(hk−1, e)

That setup lets me distinguish depth from weights. If probe accuracy rises late in the trajectory, the change cannot be explained by switching to a larger model or a different checkpoint. Something about the evolving state has changed.

But decodability alone is weak evidence. A probe can exploit information the base model never uses. That is why the second half of the experiment uses activation patching: I take a state from a paired counterfactual example, insert it into the recipient run, resume computation, and measure whether the answer moves toward the donor.

Experimental design

I generated 500 synthetic policy problems arranged into 250 counterfactual pairs. Every problem has a base rule and a possible exception. The model has to follow a short relation chain, inspect a property at the endpoint, and decide whether the exception reverses the base decision.

Because I generate the task, I know the latent variable I want to test: which decision path actually governs the answer—base rule or exception? I balance that path label against the final allow/deny answer so that a classifier cannot score well simply by learning the output label.

I use 350 examples for training, 76 for validation, and 74 for the held-out test set. For each example and recurrence depth, I save the answer scores, generated rationale, and the last prompt token’s hidden state after every loop. Runs for the same example share a seed, so the prefix of a deeper run matches the corresponding shallower run.

The analysis has three components:

  1. Linear state probes. L2-regularized logistic classifiers predict the path label from recurrent activations. Prelude and loop-0 states serve as pre-recurrence baselines.
  2. Rationale monitors. A TF–IDF classifier and a frozen MiniLM encoder test how much of the same path label is recoverable from the generated explanation. Lines containing FINAL: are removed before monitoring.
  3. Counterfactual activation patching. A recipient receives the saved state from its paired donor at a chosen loop. I measure donor-directed flips and changes in the answer-logit margin, alongside self, unrelated-donor, and same-answer controls.
Why the train / validation / test split matters

The probe’s regularization setting is chosen on validation data, then the selected configuration is refit on training plus validation examples and evaluated once on the held-out test set. With 74 test examples, a small number of predictions can move the reported score materially, which is why I show uncertainty intervals rather than only point estimates.

Where the signal appears

Before recurrence, I do not recover the decision path above chance: the prelude probe is at 50.0% balanced accuracy and the initialized state is at 47.3%. In the 32-pass run, however, decodability rises sharply in the middle-to-late trajectory. It reaches 91.9% at loop 16, peaks at 94.6% at loop 18, and ends at 86.5%.

I find the shape of that trajectory more informative than the peak alone. The label is not simply present at initialization and carried forward. A linearly accessible representation emerges after repeated computation. Whether Huginn itself uses that representation is a separate question.

Balanced accuracy

Balanced accuracy is ½(recallbase + recallexception). A constant classifier scores 50%. Here it measures an external probe’s ability to recover the decision path; it is not Huginn’s task accuracy.

Hidden state versus written rationale

The rationale monitors do not show the same late-depth gain. At 16 and 32 passes, both text monitors remain far below the internal-state probe, and their 95% intervals include chance at every tested depth.

This is the result that originally motivated the “where does the reasoning go?” framing, but I now think that wording is too strong. The experiment shows an observability gap between these specific monitoring methods. It does not establish that the hidden state contains a faithful internal narrative while the rationale hides it.

The distinction is especially important in light of the GPT-6 discussion above. OpenAI’s CoT monitorability work evaluates access to model reasoning traces that are different from the elicited rationale I generate here. My result is therefore evidence about this model, this task, and these observation channels—not a direct estimate of GPT-6-style CoT monitorability.

The most important sanity check is directly underneath the bars: at 16 passes, Huginn itself answers only 50.0% of the policy problems correctly. A 91.9% probe score therefore cannot be read as “Huginn reasons correctly 91.9% of the time.” The probe and the base model are solving different prediction problems.

Does the decoded state actually matter?

Probing establishes correlation with a label. Patching asks for something closer to causality. For each counterfactual pair, I take the donor’s hidden state at a selected loop, replace the recipient’s state at the same location, let recurrence continue, and test whether the recipient’s answer shifts toward the donor.

I compare that intervention with self-patching, unrelated donors, and same-answer donors. The primary behavioral endpoint is a donor-directed answer flip; I also track the raw answer-logit margin so smaller shifts are not discarded.

I observe zero donor-directed flips across the tested loops. There are small logit-margin shifts, but they do not cleanly separate from the unrelated-donor control. This is a null causal result under a limited intervention—not evidence that the recurrent state is behaviorally irrelevant in general.

What this does—and does not—show

The cleanest statement I can support is: a task-relevant label becomes highly linearly decodable from later Huginn states, while I do not demonstrate that the decoded information drives the model’s answer. That is why the paper uses the phrase late linear decodability without demonstrated behavioral use.

The biggest limitation is task competence. Huginn is near chance on the underlying policy task, which makes a failed causal intervention difficult to interpret. The next experiment should start from a task the model solves reliably, then repeat the state/rationale comparison across multiple seeds and datasets, use pair-clustered uncertainty, and check more aggressively for lexical shortcuts.

The intervention should also become more targeted. Replacing an entire token state perturbs many features at once. A better follow-up would identify a lower-dimensional direction or subspace associated with the path label, intervene along that feature, and test whether the effect survives appropriate random-direction and matched-norm controls.

This is also where the GPT-6 connection becomes most useful. OpenAI’s Astra work argues that future oversight may need signals beyond verbalized reasoning, including activation-based monitoring. My result does not solve that problem, but it gives me a concrete next research program: move from “is information decodable?” to “can an activation-level monitor recover behaviorally relevant information robustly enough to matter for oversight?”
